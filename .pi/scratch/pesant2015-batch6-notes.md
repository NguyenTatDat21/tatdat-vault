## idx 36 — Two Clause Learning Approaches for Disjunctive Scheduling (Mohamed Siala (B), Christian Artigues, Emmanuel Hebrard; Insight Centre UCC / CNRS LAAS Toulouse; source lines 11,046-11,261, paper pp. 393-402, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-23219-5 28; footnote lines 11,286-11,288)
- Abstract verbatim: revisits the standard hybrid CP/SAT approach for disjunctive scheduling. Previous methods entail creating redundant clauses when lazily generating atoms standing for bounds modifications. First, an alternative method for handling lazily generated atoms without computational overhead; second, a novel conflict analysis scheme tailored for disjunctive scheduling. Experiments on well-known Job Shop Scheduling instances show compelling evidence of the efficiency of the proposed learning mechanism; in particular very efficient for proving unfeasibility.
- Contributions: hybrid CP/SAT built on the "light model" (minimalist propagation + weighted degree). Mimics CDCL, but differs from Lazy Clause Generation in two ways. (1) Because the time horizon is large, bound literals must be generated lazily during conflict analysis; handling domain consistency through clauses is redundant, so a dedicated **DomainFaithfulness** propagator runs in constant amortized time. (2) A novel conflict analysis scheme tailored for disjunctive scheduling, applicable to any problem whose search can be restricted to a predefined set of Boolean variables — once every pair of tasks sharing a resource is sequenced, a complete solution is found in polynomial time, so search branches only on relative order of tasks on a resource. Resolution continues until the nogood contains only Boolean ordering variables, so no domain atoms are generated. Experiments show that not generating atoms outweighs the richer literal language of traditional hybrid solvers, and the novel approach is very efficient especially for proving unfeasibility, improving lower bounds on several well-known JSSP benchmarks (the IBM CP-Optimizer method [25] generally finds better bounds).
- Section 2 background (hybrid CP/SAT / LCG notation): domain ops `[[x=v]]`, `[[x≠v]]`, `[[x≤v]]`, `[[x≥v]]`; each literal p has `level(p)` (nodes in the current branch when p becomes true) and `rank(p)` (domain ops at the same node before p); implication `wp ⇒ p` where `wp` = explain(p), computed forward (clausal) or backward on demand. Algorithm 1 `1-UIP-with-Propagators`: `Ψ ← explain(⊥)`; while `|{q∈Ψ | level(q)=current level}| > 1` pick `p ← arg max_q{rank(q) | level(q)=current level ∧ q∈Ψ}`, then `Ψ ← Ψ ∪ {q ∈ explain(p) | level(q)>0} \ {p}`; return Ψ; backjump to `l = max(level(p_i))`. Lazy atom generation for `[[x≤u]]` requires adding clauses `¬[[x≤a]] ∨ [[x≤u]]`, `¬[[x≤u]] ∨ [[x≤b]]` (a,b nearest generated bounds, a<u<b), making `¬[[x≤l]] ∨ [[x≤u]]` redundant.
- Section 3 CP model for JSSP: n jobs on m machines, task `T_ik` = job `J_i` on machine `M_k`, duration `p_ik`, start variable `t_ik`, processing interval `[t_ik, t_ik+p_ik)`; O(n^2) Boolean variables `δ_kij` (i<j) per machine with Eq. (1): `δ_kij = 0 ⇔ t_ik + p_ik ≤ t_jk ; 1 ⇔ t_jk + p_jk ≤ t_ik`; job precedence constraints; objective variable `C_max`. Search: greedy upper bound, then dichotomic search on `C_max` (each step a fixed time cutoff; exceeding it is read as unsat), then branch-and-bound to close the gap. Branching on the disjunctive Booleans per [13] with solution-guided value ordering [3], weighted-degree heuristic `taskDom/tw` [10,13] plus VSIDS [16].
- Section 4 lazy generation: **DomainFaithfulness(x, [b_1..b_n], [v_1..v_n])** = ∀i, `b_i ↔ x ≤ v_i`, with `v_i < v_{i+1}`. Role: (a) simulate unit propagation as if atoms were eagerly generated (`b_i=1` ⇒ `b_{i+1}=1`; `b_i=0` ⇒ `b_{i-1}=0`); (b) complete channeling between the range variable and all generated atoms. Two cases for the upper bound: atom `b_i=1` may set the upper bound to `v_i` (failure if `v_i < min(x)`); on an upper-bound event with value u, every atom with `v_i ≥ u` is set to 1 — let `i_ub = argmax_k(v_k | D(b_k)={1})`, `last_ub = argmin_k(v_k | v_k ≥ u)`, and assign every atom in `φ = {b_{i_ub}, ..., b_{last_ub}}` to 1. Because the upper bound only decreases down a branch, `i_ub` is computed by scanning successive upper bounds from where it stopped, so each atom is visited at most once per branch: constant amortized time, O(n) per branch, storing `i_ub` and `i_lb` as reversible integers.
- Section 5 learning restricted to task ordering: every bound literal p with `level(p)>0` has a non-empty explanation, so search only branches on disjunctive Booleans. Compute the 1-UIP nogood Ψ, then keep explaining the latest literal in Ψ until it is not a bound literal (terminates at the last decision) → `Ψ*`. Second phase `Substitute(S, Ψ)` (Algorithm 2) with `S = ` bound literals of `Ψ*`: `p ← choose p∈S`; `visited ← visited ∪ {p}`; `Ψ ← Ψ ∪ {q ∈ explain(p) | level(q)>0} \ visited`; `S ← {q ∈ Ψ | q is a bound literal}`; return Ψ — a nogood with only Boolean variables. Backjump level is unchanged. No atoms are generated, so domain/atom consistency need not be maintained, greatly reducing the impact of the scheduling horizon size; a slight overhead in conflict analysis (more literals to explain) and shorter proofs may be possible in the standard approach because its literal language is richer.
- Section 6 experiments: models implemented in Mistral-2.0, Intel Xeon E5-2640. `CP(task)` = CP model with taskDom/tw; hybrid `H(θ,σ)`, `θ ∈ {vsids, task}`, `σ ∈ {lazy, disj}`. Benchmarks Lawrence [14] and Taillard [23]; 10 runs per method with randomized geometric restarts [26]; each dichotomy step ≤ 300 s and 4×10^6 propagation calls; total 3600 s per configuration. Table 1 (mean %O, T, nds/s, PRD): la-01-40 + tai-01-10 — CP(task) 87% 52289 nds/s; H(vsids,disj) 91.5% 43790; H(vsids,lazy) 88% 63288; H(task,disj) 90.5% 43490; H(task,lazy) 88.75% 50984. PRD rows (small→best) — tai-11-20: CP 1.8432, H(vsids,disj) 1.1564, H(vsids,lazy) 1.3725, H(task,disj) 1.2741, H(task,lazy) 1.2824; tai-21-30 1.6131/0.9150/1.0841/0.9660/0.8745; tai-31-40 5.4149/4.0210/3.7350/4.0536/3.8844; tai-41-50 7.0439/4.8362/4.6800/4.9305/5.0136; tai-51-60 3.0346/3.2449/3.7809/1.1156/1.1675; tai-61-70 6.8598/6.5890/5.4264/3.9637/3.6617. Conclusions: learning scheme `disj` dominates on small/medium instances; clause learning matters on large instances; branching choice matters most on the largest instances (tai-51-60: PRD 1.1156 for H(task,disj) vs 3.2449 for H(vsids,disj)); CP(task) is faster in nds/s than hybrid models (propagating learnt clauses is an overhead); lazy slows search considerably vs disj; `disj` learns shorter clauses — on tai-11..20 with VSIDS average clause size 31 vs 43 (several bound literals share part of their explanations). Hybrid models outperform the CP model in most cases; no new upper bounds for hard instances; best model deviates only a few percent from best known bounds. State-of-the-art CP method [25] uses a 30000 s timeout with 600 s per iteration in the bottom-up phase, best-known bounds as extra information, and double threading; this paper uses a single thread and 3600 s per instance.
- Section 6.2 lower bounds: modified dichotomy (a cutoff-reaching step is assumed SAT), 1400 s per step, 3600 s overall, over the 22 open Taillard instances: 7 new lower bounds — tai13 1305 (old 1282), tai21 1613 (1573), tai23 1514 (1474), tai25 1544 (1518), tai26 1561 (1558), tai29 1576 (1525), tai30 1515 (1485). Most of these were already improved by Vilím et al. [25] using best known bounds as extra information before search. Excluding the set tai40-50, the best combination H(vsids,disj) with Luby restarts [15] finds lower bounds 2.78% lower on average than the best known published bounds. With 2500 s per dichotomy step and 7200 s global: new lower bound 1583 for tai-29 (previously 1573) and 1528 for tai-30 (previously 1519).
- Conclusion: the two hybrid CP/SAT approaches fit the CDCL framework; similar techniques exist in Chuffed and other hybrid solvers, unpublished; the novel learning scheme is extremely efficient for proving unfeasibility and improved the best-known lower bounds of two Taillard instances. ## idx 38 — Restricted Path Consistency Revisited (Kostas Stergiou (B), Department of Informatics and Telecommunications Engineering, University of Western Macedonia, Kozani, Greece; source lines 11,624-11,875, paper pp. 419-428, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-23219-5 30; footnote lines 11,641-11,643)
- Abstract verbatim: restricted path consistency (RPC), a strong local consistency for binary constraints proposed 20 years ago, was identified as a promising alternative to arc consistency (AC) in an early experimental study, and has been neglected ever since, unlike SAC and maxRPC. Proposes RPC3, a new lightweight RPC algorithm that is very easy to implement and can be efficiently applied throughout search, plus a wide experimental study of RPC3 and a light version approximating RPC, compared to state-of-the-art AC and maxRPC. Results: restricted RPC is by far more efficient than both AC and maxRPC when applied throughout search, strongly suggesting the established perception that MAC is the best general-purpose method for binary CSPs should be reconsidered.
- Background: RPC = a value is AC and for each constraint where the value has a single support, the pair is path consistent (PC) [Berlandier 1995]; maxRPC = AC and there exists a support such that the pair is PC [Debruyne & Bessière 1997]. PC-support and PC-witness definitions given. Prior RPC algorithms RPC1 (AC4-based) and RPC2 (AC6-based) use heavy data structures too expensive to maintain during search, with complexities O(ned^3) and O(ned^2) and space O(ed^2) and O(end); light residue-based AC3^r and lmaxRPC3^r established that light approximations are the best choice inside MAC.
- Algorithm RPC3: coarse-grained AC3-like structure with a FIFO propagation list of constraint pairs, storing for each `c_ij` and value `a_i` two residues R1 and R2 = the two most recently discovered supports of `a_i` in `D(x_j)`, initialized to NIL (which precedes all values). If both residues are valid, skip; if exactly one is valid, `R` = the valid residue, else NIL; `findTwoSupports(x_i,a_i,x_j,R)` searches D(x_j) for two supports, updating R1/R2; on no support, delete `a_i`; on one support `a_j`, check PC — for each third variable `x_k` constrained with both, first try residues (a valid residue consistent with `a_j` proves a PC-witness without scanning D(x_k)), else iterate D(x_k). Enqueueing rules: pairs `(x_k,x_i)` after deletions from `D(x_i)` give pruning stronger than AC; the restricted variant **rRPC3** enqueues only such pairs, achieving a local consistency weaker than RPC but stronger than AC, and considerably faster in practice; full RPC additionally enqueues pairs `(x_l,x_k)` for `x_l` constrained with `x_i` because the last PC-witness in `D(x_i)` for a pair of values on `x_k,x_l` may have been deleted. Worst-case time O(ned^3), space O(ed) residues, and O(ed) inside search — no expensive data-structure restoration after failures, unlike RPC1/RPC2.
- Experiments: 17 classes of binary CSPs from C. Lecoutre's XCSP repository (rlfap, graph coloring, qcp, qwh, bqwh, driver, job shop, haystacks, hanoi, pigeons, black hole, ehi, queens, geometric, composed, forced random, model B random), 1142 instances total; dom/wdeg heuristic for variable ordering, lexicographic value ordering; machine FUJITSU PRIMERGY RX200 S7 R2, Intel Xeon E5-2667 2.90 GHz, 48 GB ECC RAM, 16 MB cache. Algorithms compared: AC (AC3^r + dom/wdeg = MAC baseline), rRPC (rRPC3 during search), RPC (RPC3 during search), maxRPC (lmaxRPC3^r). Timeout 3600 s. rRPC only timed out on instances where AC and maxRPC also timed out; several instances solved by rRPC within the limit where AC and/or maxRPC timed out; RPC timed out on a few instances rRPC did not, the opposite never occurred. rRPC is more efficient in mean run time on all structured classes except queens, with dramatic margins on qwh and qcp; in all instances of 16 classes AC was at most 1.7× faster than rRPC; 7 rlfap instances where AC timed out and rRPC finished (mean 110 s), 12 graph coloring instances (rRPC 1.8-1798 s); rRPC orders of magnitude faster is common in qcp, qwh, graph coloring, bqwh, haystacks, ehi. On random problems rRPC ≈ AC on geometric, slower on forced random and model B random (not significant); queens is the only class with significant advantage for AC (up to 5×, and AC orders of magnitude faster than RPC/maxRPC on dense structured problems). Table 1 mean values (AC / rRPC / RPC / maxRPC, node visits, seconds): rlfap (24) 29045/64.1, 11234/32.3, 10878/39.0, 8757/134.0; qcp (60) 307416/345.4, 37725/44.8, 43068/167.1, 49005/101.3; qwh (40) 205232/1348.2, 20663/46.2, 28694/177.9, 24205/64.7; bqwh (200) 28573/7.6, 7041/2.4, 5466/2.9, 6136/2.7; graph coloring (177) 322220/88.6, 261882/60.4, 192538/73.7, 263227/138.79; geometric (100) 111611/58.4, 54721/58.6, 52416/97.3, 38227/190.9; forced random (20) 348473/143.5, 197994/177.2, 191114/309.8, 154903/455.4; model B random (40) 741124/194.3, 383927/224.5, 361926/346.5, 28871/1044.9; queens (14) 2092/14.2, 797/59.2, 2476/1032.2, 953/2025.2. Wins (excluding trivial): rlfap AC 1, rRPC 18, RPC 1, maxRPC 0; qcp 2/8/0/4; qwh 0/9/0/6; bqwh 2/36/13/20; graph coloring 8/35/17/0; geometric 12/11/1/0; forced random 20/0/0/0; model B random 39/1/0/0; queens 4/0/0/0. Timeouts where rRPC finished: AC 7 rlfap, 12 graph coloring, 14 maxRPC in model B, 1 maxRPC in queens. Observation for further investigation: on soluble instances rRPC sometimes visits fewer nodes than RPC/maxRPC despite weaker pruning, suggesting interaction with dom/wdeg guides search to solutions faster. Classes not in Table 1: composed and hanoi all trivial; black hole and job shop trivial or very hard; ehi seconds for AC, under a second for the others; haystacks very hard except a few (haystacks-04 AC 8 s vs 0.2 s for the other three); pigeons trivial/very hard, rRPC best then AC on a few (pigeons-12 AC 709 s, rRPC 550 s, RPC and maxRPC timeout); driver 7 instances, 3 trivial, rRPC best on 3, AC best on 1.
- Conclusion: RPC was recognized as promising but neglected for ~15 years; RPC3 uses residues (now standard for AC and maxRPC implementations) and the first wide experimental study of RPC inside search shows rRPC3 is by far more efficient than state-of-the-art AC and maxRPC during search, challenging the perception that MAC is the best general-purpose solver for binary CSPs.

## idx 39 — Machine Learning of Bayesian Networks Using Constraint Programming (Peter van Beek (B) and Hella-Franziska Hoffmann, Cheriton School of Computer Science, University of Waterloo; source lines 11,876-12,246, paper pp. 429-445, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-23219-5 31; footnote line 11,890)
- Abstract verbatim: learning a Bayesian network from discrete data is a combinatorial optimization problem with much prior work (ILP, A* search, depth-first BnB, breadth-first BnB). Presents a constraint-based depth-first BnB approach with an improved constraint model containing powerful dominance constraints, symmetry-breaking constraints, cost-based pruning rules, and an acyclicity constraint for pruning the search for a minimum-cost solution. Evaluated on a representative suite of benchmark data; empirical results compare favorably to the best previous approaches both in number of instances solved within resource bounds and in solution time.
- Problem: BN = labeled DAG over random variables V with a CPD at each vertex; score-and-search with a decomposable scoring function (BIC/MDL, BDeu), `σ(G|I) = Σ_i σ(v_i, parents(v_i) | I)`, local scores precomputed; worst case 2^(n-1) parent sets per variable; NP-hard even with ≤2 parents per vertex [2], unlikely to be efficiently approximatable, motivating global exact search over local heuristic search.
- Model: vertex variables `v_i` with domain = possible parent sets (cost per value, minimize total cost); ordering (permutation) variables `o_i` with `alldifferent(o_1..o_n)`; depth variables `d_i` with `dom(d_i) = {0..n-1}` where `d_i = k` is the length of the longest path from a source to the vertex at position i. Constraints (1) `acyclic(v_1..v_n)` global; (2) `alldifferent`; (3) ∀j∀p: `v_j = p ⇔ ∃!i • o_i = j ∧ d_i = depth(p | o_1..o_{i-1})`; (4) symmetric channeling from ordering to vertex variables; Definition 2 gives depth(p|prefix) = 0 if p = ∅, 1 + max depth of the elements of p if each element is in a parent set of a vertex earlier in the ordering, ∞ otherwise; propagation restricted to two easy special cases (all of o_1..o_{i-1} instantiated, or some instantiated and all p ∈ dom(v_j) subsets of them); bounds are maintained on depth variables. The vertex variables + acyclicity alone suffice (Bartlett & Cussens ILP); the permutation space alone suffices (Larrañaga et al., Teyssier & Koller local search, dynamic programming [4-6], A* ordering graph [9-11], DFBnB [13], BFBnB [10,11,15]) — the unique aspect is combining the DAG and permutation spaces and adding depth variables.
- Symmetry breaking (I): `d_1 = 0` (5); `d_i = k ⇔ (d_{i+1} = k ∨ d_{i+1} = k+1)` (6, a dominance constraint); `d_i = d_{i+1} ⇒ o_i < o_{i+1}` (7, symmetry breaking); Theorem 1 — any ordering prefix whose depth variables violate Constraints 5-7 can be safely pruned. Symmetry breaking (II): vertex variables v_1, v_2 are symmetric if `[v_1/v_2]dom(v_1) = dom(v_2)`; break by requiring `o_i = 1 ∧ o_j = 2 ⇒ i < j` (8). Symmetry breaking (III): I-equivalence via Chickering's covered-edge characterization (Definition 3: edge x→y is covered iff parents(y) = parents(x) ∪ {x}; Theorem 2: reversing x→y yields an equivalent DAG iff it is a covered edge); three cases of covered-edge reversal sequences (Case 1 keep pair iff i<j; Case 2 keep triple iff i<k; Case 3 keep quadruple iff i<l); symmetries are broken only when the two I-equivalent DAGs have equal cost, otherwise there is a negative interaction with cost-based pruning; used as a satisfiability check at each node because propagating them did not improve runtime.
- Dominance: Theorem 3 (given ordering prefix, a value p consistent with the ordering and cost(p) ≤ cost(p') ⇒ p' pruned); Theorem 4 (Teyssier & Koller preprocessing rule: p ⊂ p' and cost(p) ≤ cost(p') ⇒ p' pruned); generalized to search via induced parent sets `ip(p)` (Theorem 5: p ⊆ ip(p') and cost(p) ≤ cost(p') ⇒ p' pruned); Theorem 6 (an ordering prefix can be pruned if some permutation of it has lower cost) with three strategies — Strategy 1 permutations differing in the last l ≤ 4 places; Strategy 2 swapping the last variable with an earlier one; Strategy 3 memoization of ordering prefixes with hashing and quadratic probing, keeping the most recent entry when full, continuing only if the prefix has a better cost than one explored.
- Acyclicity: Theorem 7 (G is acyclic iff every non-empty subset S ⊆ V has a vertex v with parents(v) ∩ S = ∅); Algorithm 1 iteratively builds sets W^0 (vertices whose domain contains ∅), W^1 (vertices with a possible parent set of possible sources only), etc., returning false if some W^k is empty or the remaining set is contradictory; Theorem 8 — satisfiability of the acyclic constraint in O(n^2 d) time, which is used to achieve generalized arc consistency. Example 14: four variables with domains {{B},{D}}, {{A},{C}}, {{B},{D}}, {{A},{C}} returns false because W^0 = ∅.

## idx 40 — Hybridization of Interval CP and Evolutionary Algorithms for Optimizing Difficult Problems (Charlie Vanaret (B), Jean-Baptiste Gotteland, Nicolas Durand, Jean-Marc Alliot; IRIT Toulouse / ENAC Toulouse; source lines 12,247-12,661, paper pp. 446-462, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-23219-5 32; footnote line 12,262)
- Abstract verbatim: the only rigorous approaches for a numerical proof of optimality in global optimization are interval-based methods interleaving branching and pruning; state-of-the-art solvers integrate local optimization to compute a good upper bound. Proposes a cooperative framework where interval methods cooperate with evolutionary algorithms; within the cooperative solver **Charibde** the EA and the interval algorithm run in parallel and exchange bounds, solutions and search space via message passing. Comparison with interval solvers (GlobSol, IBBA, Ibex) and NLP solvers (Couenne, BARON) on a benchmark of difficult COCONUT problems shows Charibde is highly competitive against non-rigorous solvers and converges faster than rigorous solvers by an order of magnitude.
- Problem (P): `min_{x∈D⊂R^n} f(x)` s.t. `g_i(x) ≤ 0`, `h_j(x) = 0` over a hyperrectangular domain; non-convex f, g, h ⇒ multiple local minima; exhaustive branch-and-bound with enclosure methods keeps the best known upper bound `f̃` of the global optimum `f*` and prunes subspaces that cannot contain a global minimizer. Hybridization taxonomy [24]: integrative (one method replaces an operator of the other — GA inside interval BB providing the split direction [34], crossover replaced by BB [9]) vs cooperative (independent methods run sequentially or in parallel — interval BB + GA sequentially with population seeded from remaining boxes [28]; BB + memetic [12]; beam search + memetic [7]; GA + interval BB exchanging upper bounds and solutions through shared memory [1], which had limited efficiency/reliability, naive interval techniques that may lose solutions, and a GA that may send evaluations subject to roundoff errors). This paper builds on [1], pairing a stochastic differential evolution (DE) with a deterministic interval branch-and-contract (IBC) algorithm.
- DE: population size NP, amplitude factor W, crossover rate CR ∈ [0,1]; for each individual x, three distinct individuals u (base), v, w are picked; Eq. (2): `y_i = u_i + W × (v_i − w_i)` if `i = R` or `r_i < CR`, else `x_i`; R ensures at least one coordinate differs; y replaces x if better. The difference v−w scaled by W approximates the direction opposite the gradient. Base-individual strategy: index obtained by summing x's index and an offset in {1..NP−1} uniformly, so every individual is the base exactly once per generation. Bounceback bound handling (Eq. 5): if `y_i` exceeds a bound, replace with `u_i + ω(overline{D_i} − u_i)` or `u_i + ω(underline{D_i} − u_i)` with ω uniform in [0,1]. Direct constraint handling: compare x and y by (feasible and y no worse), (y feasible, x not), (both infeasible, y violates no constraint more than x). Rigorous feasibility: constraints evaluated with interval arithmetic; x is feasible iff `∀i, overline{G_i(x)} ≤ 0` — tolerating relaxed constraints `g ≤ 1e-6` is not a rigorous upper bound.
- Interval arithmetic: outward rounding (left bound toward −∞, right toward +∞) for conservative enclosures; Definition 1 (interval extension/inclusion function F with `f(X) ⊂ F(X)` and monotonicity `X ⊂ Y ⇒ F(X) ⊂ F(Y)`); natural extension F_N and Taylor extension F_T at c (Example 1: f(x) = x²−x, X = [−2,0.5], exact range [−0.25,6], F_N(X) = [−0.5,6], F_T(X,c) = [−5.5,7]); the dependency problem (Example 2: X−X = [−10,10] ⊃ {0}) and interval extensions of different convergence orders. Contractors used: HC4Revise (two-phase: interval evaluation over the syntax tree, then projection-function inverse; invoked as the revised subcontractor of the AC3-like HC4 loop) and X-Newton (outer linear relaxation of objective and constraints, LP lower bound via simplex, 2n additional calls contract domains); other state-of-the-art contractors are HC4, Box, Mohc, 3B, CID, X-Newton.
- Charibde: Cooperative Hybrid Algorithm using Reliable Interval-Based methods and Differential Evolution, written in OCaml; fully rigorous despite the stochastic component. Cooperation is three steps: (1) when DE improves its best evaluation, send the best individual and evaluation to IBC to update `f̃`; (2) when IBC finds a better punctual solution (e.g. the box midpoint), inject it into DE's population; (3) the MaxDist exploration strategy periodically reduces DE's search space and regenerates the population in the new domain. Two processes exchange bounds, solutions, and search space by MPI message passing (Algorithm 1 DE: MPI_ReceiveIBC/Insert/MPI_SendIBC; Algorithm 2 IBC: priority queue Q, contract w.r.t. constraints, midpoint test `overline{F(m(X))} < f̃` ⇒ update f̃ and MPI_SendDE, split X into X1,X2, insert). Contributions: (1) the new cooperative exploration strategy MaxDist (selects boxes to explore in a novel manner, periodically reduces DE's domain, restarts the population within the new smaller domain, with an example showing the domain evolves without loss of solutions); (2) assessing Charibde against rigorous (GlobSol, IBBA, Ibex) and non-rigorous (Couenne, BARON) solvers on difficult benchmark problems. Preliminary 2013 results [33] on classical multimodal problems (7 bound-constrained, 4 inequality-constrained) showed Charibde benefits from DE's start of convergence, completed the convergence proof faster than standard IBC, and produced new optimal results for Rana, Eggholder, and Michalewicz.

## idx 55 — Design and Evaluation of a Constraint-Based Energy Saving and Scheduling Recommender System (Seán Og Murphy (B), Oscar Manzano, Kenneth N. Brown; International Energy Research Centre Cork + Insight Centre UCC; source lines 17,497-17,745, paper pp. 687-703, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-23219-5 47; footnote line 17,504)
- Abstract verbatim: low-cost, interoperable home sensing (Zigbee) has motivated consumer-level energy and home monitoring software, opening the home energy space to consumer-level energy optimisation with homeowners engaged with their energy-use data. Describes a tablet-based home energy cost-saving and appliance scheduling system that calculates behaviour-change suggestions that save on energy bills while avoiding disruption to regular routines; the system uses a CSP solver to compute savings from real-world sensor data and generate revised schedules in a user-friendly format, operating within a limited computing environment with fast computation times.
- Context: AUTHENTIC is a multi-institutional Home Area Network (HAN) project: Zigbee sensors in a multi-hop wireless network with a low-power gateway Linux PC storing readings in MySQL, retrievable through a REST interface as JSON, displayed by an Android Reporting Tool app. Phase-1 sensors: smartplug sensors on power sockets reporting appliance activations and consumption, PIR motion detectors for occupancy, temperature, light, humidity, and door/window contact sensors. The app visualizes total consumption, per-room, per-appliance, occupancy patterns, correlations, comparisons, real-time alerts, annual energy goals.
- Requirements: two goals (reduce cost, reduce total consumption) balanced against comfort/safety preferences; proposals must be personal/tailored; interaction load must be manageable (systems requiring extensive preference elicitation before any proposal are impractical in domestic settings); privacy respected, operating entirely in the home on limited-compute devices, no data from other homes. Proposals on two levels: (1) total number/duration of activations per appliance per tariff band; (2) a per-day schedule respecting total simultaneous load, both as minimal changes to observed behaviour. Chosen CP as the single technology for all variants because of dedicated cumulative-scheduling support and flexible side constraints. Android app limited to JDK 1.6, so the solver is **Choco 2.1.5** (JDK1.6 compatible).
- Data preprocessing: Data Analysis module interfaces with the Authentic RESTful API, discretizing Occupancy, Temperature, Light, Humidity, and Appliance Activation into arrays of samples at a configurable sampling rate for like-for-like comparison. "ActivationTuples": Appliance Name, Average Consumption Per Activation (Wh), Number of Activations per Tariff, Power Draw Profile (time series, Watts); an activation runs from the moment instantaneous demand exceeds a threshold until it drops below; appliances with variable profiles are subdivided into sub-appliances per setting.
- Appliance Use Optimisation module ("Solver"): three tariffs (high, medium, low) per day; searches for a modified pattern by moving activations to cheaper bands or reducing activation counts with a constraint on total cost; objective minimises the sum of squared differences between the historical and proposed per-tariff activation counts. Variables: InputCost, Target (e.g. 0.9 of previous cost), H/L/M price per Wh, InputAct, Cons, InputH/M/L, HAct/MAct/LAct per appliance, HDiff/Mdiff/LDiff, HCost/MCost/LCost, TotalCost, TotalDiff (objective). Constraints: `A_iHCost = A_iHAct × H`, same for M and L; `A_iHDiff = (A_iHAct − A_iInputH)²`; `A_iTotalDiff = A_iHDiff + A_iMDiff + A_iLDiff`; `A_iTotalCost = A_iHCost + A_iMCost + A_iLCost`; `A_iAct = A_iHAct + A_iMAct + A_iLAct`; `TotalDiff = −Σ A_iTotalDiff`; `TotalCost = −Σ A_iTotalCost`; `TotalCost ≤ InputCost × Target`; minimise TotalDiff. The only user input for the first solution is the required reduction, so interaction load is minimal; afterwards the user adds domain restrictions on activation counts, stored and applied to future solving instances (user critique mechanism — a user need not state a preference already satisfied).
- Scheduling module: each activation task instance is split into fixed-duration subtasks with sequencing constraints; disjunctive constraints prevent multiple instances of the same appliance running simultaneously; per-task highest permissible tariff constraints (permissive, allowing lower cost than expected); peak power threshold via a **cumulative** constraint with heights equal to power demand, height limit = max power threshold, dummy tasks representing temporary lower thresholds for variable demand schemes; abnormality minimization — a time series of per-appliance use frequency over the 7-day week at 5-minute granularity is inverted (E → F−E, F = max frequency) and used as dummy constant task variables in a second cumulative constraint whose peak is a variable to minimise, lower bound = max inverted frequency; per-appliance heights are scaled to the gap between highest and lowest frequencies, so scheduling an activation entirely in high-frequency slots hits the lower bound and low-frequency slots hit the maximum; the objective minimises the sum of appliance cumulative heights = total abnormality. The user can add/delete tasks, extend durations, fix start times by sliding, and add limited temporal constraints, then request rescheduling.
- Evaluation on a Samsung Galaxy Tab 4 (Android 4.4.2) with three months of data from a real home deployment: savings module ≈ 900 ms, scheduler ≈ 17 s (30-minute slots). Savings under restricted freedom: savings targets 10% to 60% with freedom reduced gradually — user restrictions have little-to-no impact on time (all solutions 900-1150 ms), unsat cases take a consistent 780 ms. Scheduler: time-slot resolution 5 to 60 minutes with and without an Inter-Appliance Disjunction and a temporal link between two appliances — performance with user constraints ≈ default; 5-minute slots are extreme (up to 400 s), 20 minutes and above are reasonable (≈30 s at 20 min, 5 s at 60 min).
- Related work: supply-side approaches use MILP centrally at the utility, taking several minutes, with limited personal insight; Lim et al [17] MILP air-conditioning scheduling by scheduled meetings; Scott et al [21] MILP with "comfort"; Felfernig & Burke [12] on constraint-based recommender systems and user understanding; Ha et al [15] constraint-based demand-side scheduling on synthetic data with minutes of computation; He et al [16] market model with CP+MIP for RTP-based residential load scheduling; Tushar et al [26] game-theoretic demand-side management; Darby et al [11] and Carroll et al [9] end-user perception and smart-metering engagement; Bu et al [7] MILP adaptive home scheduling with fuzzy goal programming and discretization; de Sá Ferreira et al [20] utility-level tariff design under price-elasticity uncertainty.
- Conclusion/future work: expand deployments to room and water heating sensing and remote actuation of smart-plug appliances (automatically implementing the schedules), expand the peak-load model to temporally restricted renewables and home energy storage (e.g. an EV battery charged cheaply overnight, solar augmenting supply with weather-driven opportunistic scheduling).
