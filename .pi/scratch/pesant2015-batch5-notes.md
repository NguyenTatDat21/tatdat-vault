## idx 30 — Automated Auxiliary Variable Elimination Through On-the-Fly Propagator Generation (lines 9,216-9,623, 408)

- Pages 313-329; footnotes printed: `-c Springer International Publishing Switzerland 2015`, `G. Pesant (Ed.): CP 2015, LNCS 9255, pp. 313-329, 2015.`, `DOI: 10.1007/978-3-319-23219-5 22` (three separate footnote spans, lines 9,248-9,256 region).
- Authors: Jean-Noel Monette (B), Pierre Flener, Justin Pearson — Department of Information Technology, Uppsala University, Uppsala, Sweden.
- Abstract: model flattening introduces many auxiliary variables; eliminate auxiliary variables "occurring in exactly two constraints by replacing those two constraints by a new equivalent constraint for which a propagator is automatically generated on the fly"; "despite the overhead of the preprocessing and of using machine-generated propagators, eliminating auxiliary variables often reduces the solving time."
- The 5-step approach (verbatim list): 1. Identify frequent patterns consisting of two constraints sharing an auxiliary variable; 2. define a new constraint predicate involving all variables in the pattern except the shared auxiliary variable; 3. replace all occurrences by instantiating the new predicate; 4. generate a propagator description in the indexical language of [11] and compile it for the targeted solver; 5. solve the modified flattened model with the solver extended with the new indexical-based propagators.
- Low-level language assumed: FlatZinc [1]; solver: Gecode [7]. Essence [6] and MiniZinc [12] are the high-level languages; flattening research [14].
- Pattern formalism: signature `Y_1,...,Y_n,K_1,...,K_m` where `Y_i` are `n >= 2` variable identifiers and `K_i` are `m >= 0` constant identifiers; semantics = conjunction of two existing predicates `P_A(A_1,...,A_p)` and `P_B(B_1,...,B_q)` for some `p, q >= 2`; the reserved identifier `X` is the local existentially quantified shared variable, appearing in both argument lists. An occurrence = two constraints `C_1` and `C_2` sharing a variable `x` that appears only in `C_1` and `C_2`. Specialisation example: `P_1(Y_1,Y_2) <=> P_2(Y_1,Y_2,1)`.
- Example model (Fig. 1a MiniZinc → Fig. 1b FlatZinc, lines 9,303-9,333): `constraint w != max(y, z) | 1 <= z;` is flattened by introducing 3 auxiliary variables (`x_1 :: var_is_introduced`, `x_2 :: var_is_introduced`, `x_3 :: var_is_introduced`) and posting 4 primitive constraints (`x_1 = max(y, z)`, `x_2 <=> w != x_1`, `x_3 <=> 1 <= z`, `x_2 | x_3`).
- Flattening statistics: "For 75 of the 488 benchmark instances we used ... no auxiliary variables are introduced by flattening. For 264 instances, flattening multiplies the number of variables in the model by more than 5. For 7 instances, flattening even multiplies the number of variables by more than 100."
- Indexicals [20] core form "X in sigma"; Fig. 2 gives the PLUS propagator (3 indexical expressions + checker `X == Y + Z`, lines 9,357-9,371). The indexical compiler of [11] (Monette/Flener/Pearson, CP 2012 LNCS 7514 544-560) compiles such descriptions for several solvers including Gecode; "an indexical-based propagator uses between 1.2 and 2.7 times the time spent by a hand-crafted propagator on a selection of n-ary constraint predicates".
- Implementation name: `var-elim-idxs` (bitbucket.org/jmonette/var-elim-idxs); preprocessing Algorithm 1 (lines 9,430-9,443: parse; while most frequent pattern P do replace each occurrence, generate+compile indexical propagator; output; link propagators with the solver). Loop ends when occurrences of a most frequent pattern < 10 occurrences, or < 5% of the number of model variables; preprocessing is skipped entirely when the number of auxiliary variables is < 10 or < 5% of variables (checked with grep); "The two thresholds, 10 and 5%, have been set arbitrarily after some initial experiments."
- 84% of auxiliary variables in the 488 benchmark instances appear in exactly two constraints; variables in >2 constraints (from common subexpression elimination [13,14]) are not considered for elimination.
- Pattern construction rules (verbatim, lines 9,485-9,511): `a_i == x` → `X`; variable other than `x` → next unused `Y_k`; constant in `{-1, 0, 1, true, false}` → kept as literal; other constant → next unused `K_k`; arrays → array of same length applying rules elementwise. Third rule purpose: linear (in)equalities propagate more efficiently with unit coefficients.
- Ignored patterns (criteria specific to FlatZinc+Gecode, lines 9,521-9,537): n-ary predicates with >= 5 variable arguments (threshold 5 because no fixed-arity FlatZinc predicate has more than 4 variable arguments); predicates expected to propagate poorly vs hand-written (MiniZinc standard library global constraints, plus element, absolute value, division, modulo, multiplication); `bool2int` where the shared variable is the Boolean and the other predicate is not a pattern (keeps the Gecode-FlatZinc parser optimisation). `all different([X, Y, Z, W])` is excluded by the second criterion, `int lin eq(coeffs, [X, Y, Z, W, U], k)` by the first.
- Pattern instantiation rules (lines 9,541-9,559): X removed; `Y_k` instantiated to the matching variable; literal constants dropped; `K_k` → the constant; arrays elementwise. Sorted elements in n-ary Boolean conjunction/disjunction (only those two) prevent symmetric patterns; sorting can make occurrences disappear before instantiation (worked example `z_1 > 0 | z_2 > 0` with b_1/b_2, lines 9,571-9,583).
- Propagator generation: 4 steps — (1) translate the pattern into an indexical-syntax checker; (2) eliminate the shared variable from the checker; (3) generate the indexical propagator description from the checker; (4) compile to an actual propagator (C++ for Gecode). Elimination forms: `X = phi` substitution, and resolution on `(delta_1 | X) & (delta_2 | ~X)` → `delta_1 | delta_2` (lines 9,601-9,611). The whole transformation has more than 250 rewriting rules (lines 9,613-9,615); rules for conjunction/disjunction/equality and the UB function, `UB(phi_1 - phi_2) = UB(phi_1) minus-pointwise UB(phi_2)`; `S minus T = {s - t | s in S, t in T}` (lines 9,617-9,621). Similar to Nicolog projection constraints, generalised to n-ary constraints [18]; packaged mechanism previously used in [9,17].
- Ship-schedule example (MiniZinc Challenge 2012 model, 7ShipsMixed.dzn = 7 boats and 74 time slots): FlatZinc has 7,187 constraints and 5,978 variables, 4,848 auxiliary (81% of the variables). Patterns found (Table 1, lines 9,423-9,427): `P_0(Y_1,Y_2) ≜ (X | Y_1) & X ≜ (Y_2 = 0)` freq 892; `P_1(Y_1,Y_2,Y_3,Y_4) ≜ P_0(X,Y_4) & X ≜ (Y_1 & Y_2 & Y_3)` freq 612; `P_2(Y_1,Y_2,Y_3,Y_4,K_1) ≜ (X | ~(Y_1 & Y_2 & Y_3)) & X ≜ (Y_4 = K_1)` freq 612; `P_3(Y_1,Y_2,Y_3,K_1) ≜ (X | ~(Y_1 & Y_2)) & X ≜ (Y_3 = K_1)` freq 276; `P_4(Y_1,Y_2,Y_3,Y_4) ≜ (X | Y_1) & X ≜ (Y_2 & Y_3 & Y_4)` freq 146. The Algorithm-1 loop ends on `P_4` (<5% of variables, not instantiated); 4 new predicates P_0-P_3 introduce, eliminating 2,392 variables = 40% of the variables; 222 eliminated via pattern specialisation (e.g. P_2 with K_1 = 1). Final model: 3,586 variables (2,456 auxiliary), 4,795 constraints (1,780 using generated propagators); many not eliminated (more than two constraints, infrequent patterns, n-ary n >= 5, multiplication, bool2int). Fig. 3 = generated indexical description for P_1 (lines 9,429-9,447: `0==Y_4 or (1 <= Y_1 and 1 <= Y_2 and 1 <= Y_3)`, `once(not 0 memberof dom(Y_4))`, `Y_1 in 1 .. sup` etc.); the compiler treats Booleans as integers with domain {0,1}; generated C++ propagator code is 579 lines long. Unmodified Gecode solves in 111.5 seconds; var-elim-idxs in 89.1 seconds = 4.9 preprocessing + 84.2 solving.
- Experiments: preprocessing implemented in Scala using the indexical compiler (bitbucket.org/jmonette/indexicals) and the FlatZinc parser of the OscaR project; Gecode 4.4.0 and the MiniZinc flattener 2.0.1; VirtualBox VM running Ubuntu 14.04 LTS 32-bit, one core of a 64-bit Intel Core i7 at 3 GHz, 1 GB RAM. 489 of 500 FlatZinc instances from MiniZinc Challenges 2010-2014 tested; 11 instances excluded (flattening failed: memory / syntax; footnote: the sugiyama model, corrected in development MiniZinc); one run each, 10-minute time-out.

## idx 31 — Automatically Improving SAT Encoding of Constraint Problems Through Common Subexpression Elimination in Savile Row (lines 9,624-9,829, 204)

- Pages 330-340; footnotes printed as three spans: `-c Springer International Publishing Switzerland 2015`, `DOI: 10.1007/978-3-319-23219-5 23`, `G. Pesant (Ed.): CP 2015, LNCS 9255, pp. 330-340, 2015.` (order as printed, lines 9,648-9,664).
- Authors: Peter Nightingale (B), Patrick Spracklen, Ian Miguel — School of Computer Science, University of St Andrews, St Andrews, UK.
- Abstract: SAT formulation is vital to efficient solving; preprocessing and inprocessing operate on the lowest level representation (raw SAT clauses), where higher-level patterns are hard/costable to identify. "Our approach is different: rather than reformulate the SAT representation directly, we apply automated reformulations to a higher level representation (a constraint model) of the original problem." CSE demonstrated on models encoded to SAT; "In some cases we observe speed improvements of over 100 times."
- Essence includes integer (as well as Boolean) decision variables, infix operators on integer and Boolean expressions, and global constraints and functions [20]; reformulated model encoded to SAT via Savile Row.
- CSE in compiler construction [5] (Cocke 1970); Shlyakhter et al [23] exploited identical subformulae in quantified Boolean formulae; CSE reduces model size by removing redundant variables and constraints [1,10,19,20], creating stronger connections → stronger inference, reduced search.
- Earlier CSE applied directly to SAT formulations had limited success [29] (Yan et al., AAAI 2015, 4224-4225, common subclause elimination).
- Motivating point: reducing two constraint variables to one via CSE means the SAT variables/clauses encoding the second variable are never added; the equivalent step on the SAT formulation would require costly structure identification + equivalence proof.
- Modifications for SAT target: candidate set differs; implied constraints added to elicit common subexpressions are removed after CSE if unchanged; **described for the first time an identical CSE algorithm independent of general flattening**, allowing extraction from subexpressions that would not ordinarily be flattened and control of CSE order.
- Algorithm 1 Identical-CSE(AST, ST) verbatim, lines 9,676-9,701: newcons empty list; map empty hash table mapping expressions to lists; populateMap(AST, map); for all key in map in decreasing size order; ls = map(key); ls filtered by isAttached; if length(ls) > 1: e = head(ls), bnds = bounds(e), aux = ST.newAuxVar(bnds), newc = (e = aux), newcons.append(newc), for all a in ls replace a with copy(aux); AST = AST ∧ fold(∧, newcons). Algorithm 2 populateMap: candidates only; recursion over children.
- Identical CSE example: `x × y` three times → new decision variable a, new constraint `x × y = a`, occurrences replaced by a. Tailor (Rendl [10,20]) and MiniZinc [13,26] implement Identical CSE, tied to flattening; Savile Row's algorithm is not. CVC4 merges identical subtrees in its AST [3] — unclear whether it affects search or is a memory saving.
- Non-candidates for SAT: `x = c`, `x != c`, `x <= c`, `x < c`, `x >= c`, `x > c`, `not x` (x decision variable, c constant); atomic variables and constants are not candidates; compound expressions are candidates by default, and those encodable as a single SAT literal are excluded to avoid redundant SAT variables equal/negated.
- Decreasing size order rationale: eliminating long expressions first may obviate eliminating short ones; the new constraint keeps the original object e so further CSEs can be extracted from within e.
- Pre-processing of Identical CSE: evaluate all constant expressions, negation normal form, type-specific simplifications (`x <=> true` → `x`), commutative expressions sorted (sums) so equivalents become syntactically identical.
- Active CSE: `x = y` and `x != y` → one Boolean variable a with `a <=> (x = y)`, `x = y` replaced by a, `x != y` by `not a`. The Active CSE algorithm extends Algorithm 1: transform each candidate e (e.g. producing not e), put into normal form, sort commutative subexpressions, query map. **Savile Row 1.6.3 applies four transformations: Boolean negation, arithmetic negation, multiply by 2, multiply by -2.** Rendl implemented Boolean-negation active CSE in Tailor plus active reformulations on De Morgan's laws and Horn clauses [20]; negation normal form obviates the latter two. MiniZinc [13,26] does not implement Active CSE.
- AC-CSE: Nightingale et al [19] (finite domains) and Araya et al [1] (numerical CSP); Tailor and MiniZinc do not implement AC-CSE. Refinement: implied sum constraints deduced from global constraints (all-different, global cardinality) trigger AC-CSE; large sum constraints are cumbersome to encode in SAT, so implied sums are tested for modification after AC-CSE and removed if unchanged (not useful). Extended resolution [2] can be viewed as AC-CSE applied directly to the disjunctive clauses of a SAT formula.
- Effects on the output formula (worked example, two occurrences of `x × y` in sums, x,y ∈ {1..10}): each occurrence ordinarily gets an auxiliary variable (a_1, a_2 ∈ {1..100}) and a new constraint `x × y = a_i`; each a_i encoded with just under 200 SAT variables and ~400 clauses; each new constraint 100 clauses; Identical CSE creates a single auxiliary variable + one new constraint, saving hundreds of SAT variables and clauses. Hard to see how SAT pre/inprocessing rules identify that structure.
- Baseline encodings: order encoding for sums [28], support encoding [8] for binary constraints; several constraint types decomposed before encoding; Savile Row tutorial 1.6.3 appendix A [18].
- Four configurations: Basic (unifying equal variables, filtering domains, aggregation); Identical CSE; Identical & Active CSE; Identical, Active & AC-CSE. Benchmark = the example problems in Savile Row 1.6.3 [18]: **49 problem classes, 492 problem instances**, including EFPA [11] and car sequencing [6], Black Hole solitaire [9].
- Hardware: 32 processes in parallel, two 16-core AMD Opteron 6272 CPUs at 2.1 GHz, 256 GB RAM. SAT solver Lingeling [12] — winner of the Sequential, Application SAT+UNSAT track of SAT Competition 2014; `lingeling-ayv-86bf266-140429.zip` from http://fmv.jku.at/lingeling/; default options (inprocessing on). Times include Savile Row + Lingeling, median of 10 runs with 10 random seeds; 1-hour limit; clause limit 100 million; over-limit instances treated as timed out at 3600 s. 7 of 492 reached the clause limit with Basic, 6 with the other configurations.
- Geometric mean speed-ups: Identical CSE vs Basic **1.24**; Active & Identical vs Identical **0.98** (small average slow-down, active checks largely neutral); Identical, Active & AC-CSE over all instances **1.24**. CSE effect limited on easier instances, grows with difficulty, "in some cases by over 20 times"; small number of outliers on harder instances where performance degrades (conjecture: changed structure affects Lingeling heuristics).
- Figure 1 upper left/right, lower left/right captions (line 9,744): "Identical CSE vs Basic (upper left), Active & Identical CSE vs Identical CSE (upper right), Identical, Active & AC-CSE vs Identical & Active CSE (lower left), same vs Basic (lower right)."
- Five problem classes separated in the plots: Killer Sudoku most ambiguous (clusters above and below the break-even line); Car Sequencing and SONET — easier instances below break-even, more difficult instances exhibit speed-up; Peg Solitaire Action degraded on all seven instances; Molnars speed-up with one exception.
- Figure 2 (Identical, Active & AC-CSE vs Identical & Active CSE plotted against reduction in SAT variables, right plot a subset of the left, "Reduction in SAT variables by AC-CSE"): Killer Sudoku has a clear link between SAT variable count and speed-up quotient; all Peg Solitaire Action instances see AC-CSE increase SAT variables AND degrade performance; Car Sequencing and SONET show no correlation — "the number of SAT variables alone is a very coarse measure of difficulty."
- Case study 1 Car Sequencing [24] (CSPLib prob 001): sequence of integer variables x[1..n]; for each option at most p of any q adjacent cars; overlapping sums of length q containing `x[i] ∈ S`; global cardinality constraint [17] on x; implied constraint `sum over i of (x[i] ∈ S) = t` (t cars need the option); 80 instances from Nightingale [17]. Identical and Active CSE extract `x[i] ∈ S` appearing in several sum constraints, avoiding multiple SAT encodings of the same set-inclusion constraint; AC-CSE extracts common subexpressions among sum constraints for a given option, overlapping p-of-q constraints and the implied constraint; many instances become solvable within one hour, peak speed-up over 100 times; Basic: 13 of 80 instances time out at one hour; full CSE: only 2 timed out, the other 11 solved within one hour.
- Case study 2 SONET [16,25] (CSPLib prob 056): network design, nodes on rings, lexicographic ordering of indistinguishable rings, objective = number of node-ring connections minimised; simplified SONET with unlimited ring data capacity (Section 3 of [25]); formulation and 24 instances exactly as Nightingale et al [19]. Initial SONET formulation has no identical/active common subexpressions; decomposed lexicographic constraints have identical subexpressions exploited by Identical CSE (modest gains). Four groups of constraints: objective, connection constraints, per-ring node-count limits, symmetry breaking; apart from symmetry breaking all constraints are sums and all three groups overlap, so AC-CSE succeeds.
- Conclusion: CSE is a valuable reformulation tool; hitherto limited success for SAT [29]; "On some instances we observed improvements of SAT solver speed of over 50 times. On the car sequencing problem, for example, the peak speed increase is over 100 times. With the basic approach, 13 of 80 car sequencing instances could not be solved in one hour, whereas with the full CSE approach only two instances could not be solved."

## idx 32 — Exact Sampling for Regular and Markov Constraints with Belief Propagation (lines 9,830-10,033, 204)

- Pages 341-350; single footnote span: `-c Springer International Publishing Switzerland 2015 G. Pesant (Ed.): CP 2015, LNCS 9255, pp. 341-350, 2015. DOI: 10.1007/978-3-319-23219-5 24` (line 9,847).
- Authors: Alexandre Papadopoulos^1,2 (B), François Pachet^1,2, Pierre Roy^1, Jason Sakellariou^1,2 — ^1 Sony CSL, 6 Rue Amyot, 75005 Paris, France; ^2 Sorbonne Universités, UPMC University Paris 06, UMR 7606, LIP6, 75005 Paris, France.
- Abstract: for Markov models + Regular global constraints + unary constraints, **perfect sampling**; factor graph of binary factors combining a Markov chain and an automaton; "a simplified version of belief propagation to sample random sequences satisfying the global constraints, with their correct probability"; "Since the factor graph is linear, this procedure is efficient and exact." Applications: sequences of text or music imitating the style of a corpus, verifying validity constraints such as syntax or meter. Keywords: global constraints, unary constraints, Markov constraints, belief propagation, sampling.
- Related work: search heuristics and solution diversity [27]; optimisation techniques [11,13]; Gogate & Dechter **SampleSearch** — guaranteed uniform sampling of CSP solutions using a complete solver to reduce rejection rates [10]; Ermon et al — constraint solver as black-box oracle, uniform sampling, often better performance [8]; Markov logic networks unify probabilistic and deterministic properties [7,25]; MC-SAT [24] samples from a non-uniform distribution of satisfying assignments of a SAT formula; "The solution we propose, which focuses on a specific setting, is not derivable from such general methods, and is both tractable and exact."
- Markov chains: order-k chains equivalent to order-1 chains on a k-gram alphabet → the paper assumes order-1 chains only; probability `P(X_1) P(X_2|X_1) ... P(X_n|X_n-1)`; Markov chains trained by learning transition probabilities on a corpus; music sequences (pitch, duration, metrical position, [17]); text with a word alphabet.
- Regular constraint [22] (Pesant CP 2004 LNCS 3258 482-495) defined by automaton `A = <Q, Sigma, delta, q_0, F>`; Sigma = X (transitions labelled by Markov states); Meter [26] (Roy & Pachet AAAI 2013) constrains Markov sequences to be metrically correct, expressible as an automaton. Markov constraints with other constraints restrict the solution space [15] without distribution guarantees; for unary constraints the guarantee holds [2] (Barbieri/Pachet/Roy/Esposti ECAI 2012 vol 242 115-120); this result generalises to a set of Regular constraints; a specific implementation generated non-plagiaristic sequences [18].
- Belief propagation background: sum-product algorithm [20] (Pearl 1982); factor graph = bipartite undirected graph `G = (X, F, E)`; marginals `p_i(X_i) = sum over other variables`; tree factor graphs are polynomial and correspond to **Berge-acyclic constraint networks**, generalising [3,6,9] (Beeri/Fagin/Maier/Yannakakis J. ACM 30(3) 479-513; Dechter & Pearl AI 38(3) 353-366; Freuder J. ACM 29(1) 24-32). CSPs are graphical models with uniformly distributed solutions. Example 1 factor graph = 4 factors `p(X_1,X_2,X_3) = f_1(X_1,X_2) · f_2(X_2,X_3) · f_3(X_1,X_3) · f_4(X_3)` (Fig. 1, crop `page_364_image_2.jpg`).
- `p_target (X_1..X_n) ∝` Markov product · unary `P_1(X_1)..P_n(X_n)` if the sequence ∈ L(A), 0 otherwise (lines 9,914-9,924); `∝` = equality after normalisation; a big factor over the full sequence takes 1 when accepted, 0 otherwise (hard global constraint) → the factor graph of p_target is not a tree.
- Reformulation: `p_reg(Y_1..Y_n)` with `Y_i ∈ X × Q`, a = Markov state, q = automaton state; binary factor `f((a,q),(a',q')) ∝ P(a'|a)` if `q' = delta(q,a')`, 0 otherwise; `f_i = f` for all 1 ≤ i < n; non-zero probability sequences correspond to a walk in the automaton; unary `g_1((a,q)) ∝ P_1(a)` if `q = delta(q_0,a)` else 0; `g_n((a,q)) ∝ P_n(a)` if `q ∈ F` else 0; other unary `g_i((a,q)) ∝ P_i(a)`. Tree factor graph in Fig. 2 (crop `page_364_image_9.jpg`).
- **Theorem 1** (verbatim): "Sampling p_target is equivalent to sampling p_reg, and projecting each resulting sequence (a_1,q_1),...,(a_n,q_n) to a_1,...,a_n" (line 9,971). Proof: one-to-one correspondence, equal probabilities; A deterministic → unique state sequence q_0..q_n with q_n ∈ F (lines 9,973-9,991).
- Sum-product adaptation: factor graph has no cycle (no convergence issue), is almost a linear graph, factors are only unary and binary, procedure used only for sampling individual sequences. Algorithm 1 verbatim (lines 9,998-1,024): `m_{n-}` = g_n; for i = n-1 to 1: `m_{i-}(y) = sum over y' in X×Q of g_i(y)·f_i(y,y')·m_{i+1-}(y')` then normalise; forward phase: `p_1 = m_{1-}`, `y_1` drawn with probability p_1; for i = 2..n: `m_{i+}(y) = f_{i-1}(y_{i-1}, y)` for y ∈ Q, normalise, `p_i(y) = m_{i+}(y)·m_{i-}(y)`, draw y_i. Backward `m_{i-}` = marginal of Y_i over factors holding on Y_i..Y_n ("in the same way that arc-consistency guarantees that a value can be extended to a full instantiation"); computing marginals at each step = generalisation of arc-consistent domains to random variables; sampling one sequence bounded by `O(n·(|X||Q|)^2)`; for several sequences the backward phase is run once and the forward phase samples a new sequence each time with correct probability; justification established in [12,20] (Mezard & Montanari OUP 2009; Pearl 1982).
- Experiments/no automaton: the unary-constraint model is equivalent to [16] (Pachet/Roy/Barbieri IJCAI 2011 635-642); 16 notes, Markov order 1, style of Bill Evans, unary constraints on first/last note: old model 450 sequences/s, new model 1,200 sequences/s (almost three times more).
- Semiotic structures (symbolic higher-level patterns from manual annotation or pattern discovery [4]) stated with automata; automata for syntactic rules over word sequences.
- Meter in basic form: fixed total duration D, each value has a duration, a special padding value with null duration used only at the end; Meter = Regular: automaton `Q = {q_0..q_D}` (partial durations), transition from q_o1 to q_o2 labelled e iff `o_2 = o_1 + d(e)`, `F = {q_D}`; toy problem: fixed number of syllables = 36 (word duration = syllables), sampled ~1,100 sequences/s vs 230 sequences/s for a CP model with a single Meter constraint (almost five times more).
- MaxOrder [19] (Papadopoulos/Roy/Pachet AAAI 2014 2731-2737) limits the maximum order of generated sequences (length of exact copies from the corpus), filtered by computing an automaton and propagating Regular; intersection of Meter and MaxOrder automata imposes meter on max-order sequences.
- Evaluation: random walk in the automaton vs fixed-length belief propagation; words from Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, length 8, Markov order 1, max order < 4 (max-order automaton [19]); Oracle Java 7 on an iMac 3.4GHz Intel Core i7, 16 GB RAM; automaton computed in about 200 ms; length imposed by rejecting shorter sequences (random walk); over 20 million sequences sampled, 5 million unique; random walk average 5,500 sequences/s (non-rejected), belief propagation average 3,500 sequences/s; Regular-based CP model produced ~50 sequences/s; filtered sequences generated over 50 times: about 47,000 with random walk and about 35,000 with belief propagation; probability estimated as the sum of probabilities of all unique sequences found by either method, used for normalising. - Conclusion: tree-structured factor graph, polynomial and exact belief propagation, binary factors encoding a conjunction between the Markov model and the automaton; sampling faster than equivalent CP models; sequences sampled with exact probabilities; bridge between statistical inference and constraint satisfaction.

- Abstract verbatim facts: some optimisation problems need a random-looking solution with no apparent patterns, for fairness, anonymity, undetectability or unpredictability; randomised search is not a good general approach because constraints and objective functions may lead to solutions far from random; a constraint-based approach to finding pseudo-random solutions inspired by the Kolmogorov complexity definition of randomness and by data-compression methods; entropy constraints can be implemented in CP systems using well-known global constraints; applied to a problem from experimental psychology and to a factory inspection problem.
- Introduction motivations (verbatim applications): randomised experiments to avoid statistical bias [13]; random phylogenetic trees [14]; quasirandom low-discrepancy sequences for numerical integration and global optimisation [24]; randomised lists without repetition for experimental psychology [10]; random programs for compiler verification [6]; random scheduling of inspections for unpredictability [30].
- Drawbacks of randomised search: a model permitting unacceptable solutions is not correct — correctness should be independent of the search strategy; randomised search cannot prove a random-looking solution does not exist, nor that a solution is as random as possible; without careful design (see [9]) it makes a biased choice of solution; unbiased sampling of solutions gives no guarantee of a random-looking solution — unconstrained long sequences have high algorithmic entropy almost always [2], but a constrained problem may have mostly regular-looking solutions, and optimising an objective may also give regular solutions; examples of both phenomena in Section 3.2. Target constraint: israndom(v) forcing a vector v of variables to be random, added to a model.
- Kolmogorov/algorithmic entropy framing: in information theory randomness is a property of the data source, Shannon entropy computed from symbol probabilities [32]; in algorithmic information theory randomness is a property of a specific sequence, its Kolmogorov complexity = length of the smallest algorithm describing it; 1111111111 has the same probability as 1010110001 but lower algorithmic entropy (write 1 ten times); the paper uses "entropy" for algorithmic entropy.
- entropy(v, e) constraint impossible because algorithmic entropy is uncomputable [5]; pragmatic approach: constraints eliminating patterns exploited by well-known data-compression algorithms, combined as needed; applying compression and measuring compressed length gives an upper bound on algorithmic entropy, so excluding readily compressible solutions excludes low-entropy solutions. Experiments use the Eclipse constraint logic programming system [1].
- Section 2.1 non-uniform distributions: Huffman coding [15] and arithmetic coding [29] exploit variable-length symbol encodings. All variables share domain {0..m-1}; lower bounds l = l_0..l_{m-1} and upper bounds u = u_0..u_{m-1} on symbol frequencies define the frequency entropy constraint freq(v, m, l, u), implemented directly by the global cardinality constraint GCC(v, ⟨0..m-1⟩, l, u) [27].

- Section 2.2 repeated strings: adaptive-dictionary methods underlie Linux compress, V.24 bis, GIF, PKZip, Zip, LHarc, PNG, gzip, ARJ [31] with algorithms in [34-36]; they detect repeated k-grams and replace them by dictionary pointers; `011101011100` has two occurrences of the 5-gram `01110`. Section 4 Related Work (verbatim): [30] proposed statistical constraints to enforce certain types of randomness (solutions should pass statistical tests such as the t-test or Kolmogorov-Smirnov); the inspection plan found using the KS test (Figure 2, caption `Fig. 2. A partially-random inspection plan that passes a statistical test`, source lines 10,344-10,346, crop `page_382_image_3.jpg`) — "If this is the only test that might be applied by an observer then we are done. However, the schedule exhibits a visible pattern that could be used to predict the next inspection with reasonable certainty. The pattern is caused by the deterministic search strategy used. It might be important to find an inspection schedule that is not predictable by visual inspection, or by a machine learning algorithm. In this case statistical tests are not enough and we must also enforce randomness." Hardware verification: SAT solvers induced to generate random stimuli, a survey [16] of methods such as adding randomly-chosen XOR constraints; constraint solvers used for the same purpose, a recent example [19]; these approaches aim to generate an unbiased set of solutions, as do [6,8,9,11,12], "whereas we aim to maximise the algorithmic entropy of a single solution"; a similar result could be obtained by defining a new problem P′ whose solution is a set of solutions to the original problem P, adding entropy constraints to P′. [3] describes the software system **Mix** for generating constrained randomised number sequences, a hand-coded local search algorithm with several types of constraint useful for psychologists, including constraints very similar to the paper's freq and dict constraints (maximum repetition and pattern constraints respectively); no connection is made to Kolmogorov complexity or data compression, Mix does not use a generic constraint solver or metaheuristic, does not use differential constraints (it has other constraints the paper does not have), and it is designed for a special class of problem. The SPREAD constraint [22] has something in common with frequency constraints but a different motivation: it balances distributions of values, for example spreading the load between periods in a timetable; it has efficient filtering algorithms but does not aim to pass compression-based randomness tests. Markov Constraints [21] express the Markov condition as constraints so constraint solvers can generate Markovian sequences, applied to the generation of musical chord sequences.

Section 5 Discussion (verbatim): the proposed entropy constraints eliminate different types of pattern, leading to high-entropy solutions as estimated by compression algorithms and the Approximate Entropy function, complementary to statistical tests of the kind explored in [30]; all constraints are based on well-known global constraints and can also be implemented in MIP or SAT; instead of specifying bounds on the occurrences of symbols and k-grams the user could specify bounds on ApEn(k) for various k, "However, we believe that the former approach is more intuitive." Using constraints to represent randomness makes it easy to generate random-looking solutions with special properties — post constraints for randomness and for the desired properties, then any solution is guaranteed to satisfy both; applying entropy constraints "is something of an art involving a compromise between achieving high entropy, satisfying the problems constraints and possibly optimising an objective function". Even with few or no problem constraints one must take care not to exclude so many patterns that no solutions remain, as **Ramsey theory [25] shows that any sufficiently large object must contain some structure**; adding entropy constraints does not necessarily preserve satisfiability — if a problem has no sufficiently random-looking solutions, entropy constraints might eliminate all solutions; the advantage is that one can prove no such solutions exist, which cannot be done with randomised search; alternatively treat entropy constraints as soft constraints and search for the most random-looking solution. Solutions are only pseudorandom, not truly random: generated by restricting repeated symbols and k-grams to be incompressible to a class of data-compression algorithms; they might fail other tests of randomness important to applications, and the response is to turn those tests into additional constraints. The runs-test constraint realisation: sequence of n binary numbers with n_0 zeroes and n_1 ones (n_0 + n_1 = n); under a normal approximation (valid for n_0, n_1 ≥ 10) the expected number of runs is μ = 1 + 2 n_0 n_1 / n, and the variance is σ² = 2 n_0 n_1 (2 n_0 n_1 − n) / (n² (n − 1)) = (μ − 1)(μ − 2)/(n − 1); to test for randomness with 95 % confidence require the observed number of runs R to be within μ ± 1.96σ; implemented as a constraint on binary variables v_1,…,v_n by defining new binary variables b_i = reify(v_i = v_{i+1}) and posting μ − 1.96σ ≤ (1 + Σ_{i=0}^{n-2} b_i) ≤ μ + 1.96σ; if n_0, n_1 are not known in advance the implementation creates auxiliary integer variables n_0, n_1 and real variables μ, σ with constraints Σ v_i = n_1, n_0 + n_1 = n, μ n = n + n_0 n_1, σ²(n − 1) = (μ − 1)(μ − 2). Gambler's Fallacy objection: a solution with a visible pattern is statistically no less likely than one with no pattern, patterns are psychological artefacts — random binary sequences of length 6 give 111111 no less random than 010110, both have the same probability, and considering the latter "more random" is a form of Gambler's Fallacy (gamblers assume patterned numbers are less likely); "if we wish to convince humans (or automated software agents designed by humans) that a solution was randomly generated then we must reject patterns that appear non-random to humans. This intuition is made concrete by the ideas of algorithmic information theory [5]. We do not expect all readers to agree with our view: randomness is a notoriously slippery concept [2] whose nature is beyond the scope of this paper." Future work: find new applications, possibly with other patterns to be eliminate; devise randomness constraints from other literatures; turn randomness tests for number sequences into constraints, e.g. the **spectral tests [17]**, considered powerful, complex, conjectured unlikely to lead to efficient filtering (far from certain, an interesting research direction), and seeming better suited to very long sequences of numbers far longer than typical optimisation solutions; the simpler **Die-Hard Tests** (footnote 3, http://www.stat.fsu.edu/pub/diehard/) and **Die Harder Tests** (footnote 4, http://www.phy.duke.edu/~rgb/General/dieharder.php), distilled to three tests in [18], aimed at very long sequences of numbers, not obvious how to derive constraints from them. ## idx 37 — Bounding an Optimal Search Path with a Game of Cop and Robber on Graphs (Frédéric Simard, Michael Morin, Claude-Guy Quimper, François Laviolette, Josée Desharnais — Université Laval; source lines 11,262-11,623; paper pp. 403-418, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-23219-5 29; footnotes lines 11,286-11,288)

- Goal: bound the Optimal Search Path (OSP) objective in CP by relaxing it into a stochastic cop-and-drunk-robber game ("Copwin" bound); first CP implementation of the DMEAN bound (Martins 1993 longest-path relaxation, improved by Lau et al. 2008) and comparison against it.
- OSP definition: searcher and wanderer move on graph vertices, simultaneous moves along edges cost 1 time unit, max T steps; pod(v) = probability of detection at vertex v; poc_πt(v) = probability wanderer is at v at time t given undetected; cos_πt = Σ_{i=1..t} poc_πt[..](v_i)·pod(v_i); goal = path maximizing cos_πT (= cos*_{π0}); OSP is NP-hard; wanderer passive, Markov transition matrix M(r,r').
- CP model (from Morin et al. CP 2012, ref 10): decision vars PATH_t with domain V(G), constraint (2) (PATH_{t-1},PATH_t) ∈ E(G) via Table constraints; implicit vars POC_t(v), POS_t(v) ∈ [0,1]; (3) PATH_t=v ⇒ POS_t(v)=POC_t(v)·pod(v); (4) PATH_t≠v ⇒ POS_t(v)=0; (5) POC_t(v)=Σ M(r,r')(POC_{t-1}(r)−POS_{t-1}(r)); objective var Z = Σ_{t=1..T} max_v POS_t(v) (eq. 6, improves filtering over the double sum), maximize Z (eq. 7).
