## Abstract

We examine the joint distribution of value and profitability in international markets. Over the 1990-2020 sample period, portfolios formed at the intersection of value and profitability generate systematic return patterns: value and high-profitability portfolios substantially outperform growth and low- profitability portfolios. The return differences are present in developed markets (ex-US), emerging markets, subsets and combinations thereof (EAFE, ACWI ex-US), as well as in large and small stocks. When viewed in combination with similar such evidence from the US, the data suggest important benefits to targeting value and profitability jointly rather than piecemeal.

## 1. Introduction

Value and profitability are inextricably linked as investment strategies. Value is based on a “stock” variable that compares current market prices to book values. Profitability is based on a “flow” variable that captures expected profits from the same stock variable. The joint distribution of value and profitability matters because the stock and the flow are tied together: it is the stock that generates future flow. In the Miller and Modigliani (1961) present value identity, expected profitability (the flow) and book-to-market ratios (the stock) are joint drivers of expected returns. The formula merely codifies clear economic intuition: in purchasing or selling an asset, one considers its offering price relative to its replacement value (in this case, book value), and the expected future benefits from the asset, rather than either one in isolation. Novy-Marx (2014) takes this intuition further, reporting that portfolios formed based on value and profitability together have higher returns (and lower drawdown frequencies) than investing in these strategies individually, or side by side. Wahal and Repetto (2020) demonstrate that even modest tilts towards the joint distribution of value and profitability offers attractive risk-reward ratios for long-only investors. In their bootstrap simulations designed to mimic payoffs to investors with 5, 10, 20, and 30 year holding periods, the probability of negative payoffs drops sharply in portfolios tilted towards value and profitability, relative to the market portfolio.

The above studies are based on US data. In this paper, we examine the risk and return of the joint distribution of value and profitability in international markets. This serves two purposes. First, international data provide confidence with respect to the economic underpinnings of the strategies, potentially reinforcing the robustness of the empirical evidence. This out-of-sample testing avoids data snooping and confirmation biases that are pernicious for both asset pricing tests and for investors.<sup>2</sup> Second, from a practical perspective, the data speak directly to investors who wish to shrink home bias and invest globally.

<small><span class="docvortex-page-footnote" data-block-type="page_footnote" style="color:#6b7280">1 Bootstrapping has two clear advantages. First, investment firms often present long horizon strategy returns generated from overlapping return series. That approach is fundamentally flawed because such returns are not independent and provide a misleading sense of the true distribution of outcomes. Second, a bootstrap uses the empirical distribution of returns rather than relying on normality assumptions.</span></small>

<small><span class="docvortex-page-footnote" data-block-type="page_footnote" style="color:#6b7280">2 For example, Wahal (2019) shows that in data prior to 1963 largely unexamined by researchers, the profitability premium remains strong but there is little evidence of an investment premium.</span></small>

Much of the international investment done by US-domiciled investors is done via mutual funds, ETFs, and other investment vehicles that target developed or emerging markets, rather than individual countries or geographic regions. These investment vehicles are typically benchmarked or indexed to various ex-US indices. Morningstar Direct reports that as of September 2020, among the 100 largest such indices, \$248 billion was attached to developed markets (ex-US) indices, \$478 billion to emerging market indices, \$701 billion to EAFE indices, and \$1 trillion to ACWI ex-US indicates. If we extend the universe to include World and ACWI benchmarks, in aggregate there is almost \$2.5 trillion in assets linked to these benchmarks.<sup>3</sup> Given the sheer economic importance of these markets, we organize the evidence of value and profitability around these classifications. Fama and French (2017) study the underlying asset pricing questions in geographic regions (North America, Europe, Japan, and Asia-Pacific ex Japan), arguing that markets within these regions are integrated.<sup>4</sup> In contrast, our interest is in the implications of value and profitability directly for investors.

We employ a standard framework, forming value and profitability portfolios independently, and examining the return series generated using a joint ranking from both metrics. Over the 1990-2020 sample period, there is a clear relation between returns and value/profitability portfolios. In each value portfolio, returns increase steadily with profitability. And in each profitability portfolio, returns increase with value. But most importantly, the relation between the joint distribution of value and profitability is monotonic. This is true in both developed markets and emerging markets. Some readers may be concerned that these results might be driven by uninvestable small stocks which represent a small proportion of the aggregate market capitalization. Such concerns are unfounded – there are similar return patterns in large stocks. Perhaps most interesting is the magnitude of the return differentials. In developed markets, the low profitability growth portfolio earns -0.05 percent per month whereas the value and high profitability portfolio earns 1.12 percent per month, a spread of 1.17 percent per month. In emerging markets, EAFE, and ACWI ex-US portfolios, the equivalent spreads are 2.20, 1.33, and 1.43 percent per month respectively.

They do not include separate accounts, UCITS, and other institutional investment vehicles. Including additional regional indices (and smaller indices) brings this total closer to \$3 trillion.</span></small>

<small><span class="docvortex-page-footnote" data-block-type="page_footnote" style="color:#6b7280">4 Neither we, nor Fama and French (2017), conduct formal tests of market integration. Bekaert et al. (2011) report that capital controls in developed markets were largely removed in the 1980s, and a similar process took place for emerging markets in the 1990s. Pukthuanthong and Roll (2009) report dramatically increased levels of integration over time. These studies, as well as others, suggest that investors today need not think in terms of geographic regions.</span></small>

What are the implications of these results for investors? First, the results indicate that the US-based evidence on the joint distribution of value and profitability in Novy-Marx (2014) and Wahal and Repetto (2020) is robust – the observability of similar results in other markets suggests that the underlying economic intuition behind these strategies is both sound and agnostic to markets. Second, the data suggest that using information in the joint distribution of value and profitability provides interesting risk-return options. An investor could target components of the market with higher expected returns directly. Indeed, the Sharpe ratios of such portfolios are higher than those of pure value or pure profitability strategies in every country combination that we study. Another advantage of such “high expected return” portfolios is that they can comprise as much as 25 percent of the aggregate market capitalization. From a practical perspective, the implication is that portfolio capacity is quite high, particularly relative to value or profitability independently.

An investor could also hold the entire market portfolio, but systematically over-weight higher expected return securities (and underweight lower expected return securities). We conduct a simple exercise in which we build such “tilted” market portfolios in each region. Even with modest tilts, we observe improved performance relative to the market of between 0.72 and 1.68 percent per year. The performance improvement with respect to MSCI investable market indices is even larger: for developed markets, emerging markets, EAFE, and ACWI ex-US the improvements are 2.16 percent, 1.92 percent, 2.16 percent and 2.28 percent per year respectively. Overall, the data suggest that long-only investors, particularly those with long horizons, can benefit from a weighting schema that recognizes the dependence between value and profitability.

The remainder of the paper is as follows. Section 2 outlines the data and portfolio construction process. Section 3 describes the portfolios and their returns. Section 4 concludes. The details of the data cleansing techniques are contained in a technical appendix.

Such piecemeal approaches are both inefficient and counterproductive since both the expected returns and volatility of the return series co-vary.</span></small>

## 2. Data and Portfolio Construction

The data are derived from Compustat North America and Compustat Global, following the procedures in Bessembinder et al. (2019). Security coverage is relatively comprehensive. The time series is also long, particularly for international markets, from July 1990 to October 2020. Readers interested in the details of coverage, cleansing, and computations are referred to Bessembinder et al. (2019) as well as the technical appendix at the end of this document.

We include 49 countries in our analysis, converting all returns and market values to US dollar equivalents at prevailing exchange rates. As in Fama and French (2017), we combine securities across countries to generate well diversified portfolios. We organize our analysis into four country combinations: developed markets ex-US, emerging markets, EAFE, and ACWI ex-US, all as currently defined by MSCI. These designations are not intended to precisely mirror indices ascribed to these groups – they are merely a way of aggregating security information across countries in a manner that makes sense for investors.

The building block of our analysis consists of 4x4 grids of value and profitability. We measure book-to-market ratios in June of each year, adjusting book values for goodwill. We measure profitability using the cash based operating profitability measure of Ball et al. (2016), cleaning out operating profitability from accruals using consecutive balance sheet information, and scaling by book equity. We can report that using unadjusted book value, operating profitability, or cash based operating profitability measured using cash flow statements, does not change our inferences.<sup>7</sup> In constructing portfolios, we use all available securities with valid book-to-market ratios and cash based operating profitability. Portfolios are rebalanced in June of each year, and held from July to the following June. All portfolio returns are value-weighted. As in Fama and French (2017), size breaks (for separating large and small cap stocks) are based on the 90t percentile of the distribution.

<small><span class="docvortex-page-footnote" data-block-type="page_footnote" style="color:#6b7280">6 MSCI’s promotes and demotes countries in each of these designations over time. These changes make no difference to our results.</span></small>

<small><span class="docvortex-page-footnote" data-block-type="page_footnote" style="color:#6b7280">7 As expected, returns differences are larger using cash based operating profitability than operating profitability. The dominance of cash based operating profitability is well recognized by Ball et al. (2016) and Fama and French (2018), both of whom argue that adjusting for accruals generates higher Sharpe ratios and lower intercepts in standard asset pricing tests.</span></small>

Table 1 shows the 4x4 grid, with each cell containing the average percentage of market capitalization over the entire time series. The grid is color coded to aid interpretation: boxes in blue (in the northwest quadrant) correspond to low ranks for book-to-market ratios and profitability, boxes in the diagonal are yellow, and boxes in green (in the southeast quadrant) correspond to high ranks in value and profitability. These correspond to low expected return (“Low E(R)”), neutral, and high expected return (“High E(R)”) groups respectively.

On average, the diagonal “neutral” portfolios contain about 36 percent of the aggregate market capitalization in each of the four region groups. In the US, the diagonal group contains about 46 percent of the aggregate market capitalization (Wahal and Repetto (2020)). The percentage of market capitalization in growth and low profitability portfolios (i.e. the Low E(R) group) varies from 36.1 percent in emerging markets to 39.5 percent in the EAFE region. The value and high profitability groups contain roughly 25 to 27 percent of the aggregate market capitalization; the equivalent average for US markets is 18 percent. The highest expected return portfolio (high value and high profitability) has between 1.0 and 1.5 percent of the aggregate market capitalization.

## 3. Results

## 3.1 Portfolio Returns

Table 2 contains average returns for portfolios in the 4x4 grid for each of the groups. There are three ways to view the data in the table.

First, examining portfolio returns vertically (north to south), allows one to see variation generated by the stock variable (book-to-market ratios), holding average profitability constant. The average value premium (difference in returns between value and growth stocks) is positive in every profitability quartile and every combination of countries. For example, in developed markets, the value spread in the low and high profitability groups is 0.31 and 0.44 percent per month respectively. As is often the case, in emerging markets, the equivalent spreads are larger, over 1.16 percent per month. In EAFE and ACWI ex-US, the spreads are between those of developed and emerging markets.

Second, looking horizontally (west to east), we can see variation from the flow variable (profitability), holding average book-to-market ratios constant. The average difference in returns between high and low profitability stocks is positive in every book-to-market group. In developed markets, the difference is 0.73 percent in growth stocks and 0.86 percent per month in value stocks. As before, these returns differences are larger in emerging markets, over one percent per month. The return differences are also present in EAFE and ACWI ex-US.

Although most people are accustomed to assimilating information by reading from left to right, or in the case of East Asian languages vertically, it is best to view these data on the diagonal (northwest to southeast). That allows the reader to observe the joint effect of value and profitability. Doing so, it is immediately apparent that jointly controlling for the stock and flow variable generates variation in returns. In every diagonal, regardless of the country combinations, returns increase monotonically. Consider, for example, the central diagonal in developed markets. Average monthly returns for this diagonal increase are -0.05, 0.49, 0.76 and 1.12 percent per month respectively. Other diagonals show similar increases. And the patterns in emerging markets, EAFE and ACWI ex-US are also similar.

Panels A and B of Table 3 contain portfolio returns for small and large stocks respectively. As is often the case, return differences are accentuated in small stocks. Consider, for example, the value and high profitability portfolio in developed markets: in small stocks the average return is 1.11 percent per month, compared to 1.04 percent in large stocks. In emerging markets, the equivalent returns are 1.90 and 1.56 percent. Indeed, in every region studied, the average return on the small stock component portfolio is bigger than the equivalent large stock component portfolio. More importantly, however, the spread in returns in large stocks (Panel B) generated by the intersection of value and profitability remains economically large.

## 3.2 Practical Implications

Long-only long-horizon investors can use the distribution of returns described above in two ways: (a) by investing solely in higher expected return securities, or (b) holding a market portfolio that tilts towards higher expected return securities. Although these are portfolio engineering approaches rather than asset pricing issues, it is important to recognize the role of market clearing. Investors in aggregate must hold the market portfolio regardless of whether an investor chooses approach (a) or (b) above – other investors must hold excluded securities or reverse the tilt.

Panel A of Table 4 considers the first approach. Portfolios in the 4x4 grid are aggregated into three groups corresponding to low expected returns (in blue), neutral or diagonal portfolios (in yellow), and high expected returns (in green). Across country combinations, the average percentage of market capitalization in the high expected return group varies from 25.2 percent to 27.2 percent. From a portfolio capacity perspective, this is an important consideration. It suggests that relatively large amounts of capital can be deployed in this group of securities without adversely impacting prices. For each group, we compute the weighted average return from the constituent portfolios using beginning of period portfolio weights. The second and third rows in each country combination show average monthly returns and standard deviations. Unsurprisingly, average returns increase steadily from the low expected return group to the high expected return group. In developed (emerging) markets, the low expected return group has an average return of 0.46 percent (0.34 percent), whereas the high expected return group has an average return of 0.75 percent (1.27 percent). These return differences are economically large. Despite the differences in average returns, the standard deviations of returns of the high expected return groups are not substantially higher, and in some cases, lower. Overall the data suggest that targeting the combination of value and high profitability stocks in these markets generates attractive risk-return opportunities.

It is also useful to compare this approach to investing in pure value or pure profitability portfolios. To do so, we generate the return series for a pure value (profitability) portfolio as the weighted average return of all four portfolios in the bottom row (last column) of the 4x4 grid in Table 2. Given differences in volatilities, we report annualized Sharpe ratios for pure value, pure profitability, and the high E(R) portfolios in Panel B. In every single combination of countries, the Sharpe ratio generated by a combination of value and profitability is higher than that generated by value or profitability alone. For example, in ACWI ex-US, the pure value portfolio has a Sharpe ratio of 0.43 and the pure profitability portfolio has a Sharpe ratio of 0.40, but the combination of value and profitability has a Sharpe ratio of 0.45. These results are consistent with those reported by Novy-Marx (2013, 2014) for US markets.

A second way to construct a portfolio that exploits this information is to invest in the entire market but tilt towards the joint distribution of value and profitability. We do so using a two-step process. First, we maintain market capitalization weights in the diagonal portfolios that, on average, comprises about 36 percent of the aggregate market. Second, we allocate the residual (on average, approximately 64 percent) into the high and low expected return portfolios in the ratio of 60:40.<sup>8</sup> This weighting schema is trivially simple. Our purpose is not to optimize but to illustrate. Of course, one could deploy weighting schemes designed to fine tune the risk-return relation to the risk tolerance of an investor.

Table 5 shows average returns and standard deviations for the market portfolio (labelled MKT), constructed as the weighted average of the portfolios in the 4x4 grids, and the 60:40 tilted market portfolios (labelled Tilted MKT). The column labelled “difference” shows the spread portfolio, along with the associated t-statistic. In developed markets, the market portfolio has a return of 0.60 percent per month with a standard deviation of 4.63 percent. The tilted market portfolio has a return of 0.66 percent with a slightly lower standard deviation (4.47 percent). The return difference of 0.06 percent (or 0.72 percent per year) has a t-statistic of 1.75. In emerging markets, the differences are even starker. Tilting towards the joint distribution of value and profitability earns a premium of 0.14 percent per month (1.68 percent per year) with a t-statistic of 3.20. Average monthly return differences in EAFE and ACWI ex-US are 0.07 and 0.07 percent per month with t-statistics of 1.86 and 2.16 respectively. Despite these return differences, in three out of four regions, the standard deviations of the tilted market portfolios are lower than the market portfolio. In other words, while any tilted portfolio is expected to incur some tracking error relative to the market, the absolute volatility profiles of the tilted market portfolios and market portfolios are similar.

<small><span class="docvortex-page-footnote" data-block-type="page_footnote" style="color:#6b7280">8 The allocations to portfolios within the high and low expected return groups are based on market capitalization weights so that extreme portfolios do not receive unusually high weights.</span></small>

It is also useful to compare the tilted market portfolio to indices used by investors. To do so, we match each region with its equivalent MSCI Investable Market Index.  We use US dollar unhedged net returns. The time series starts in June 1994 so we also report return statistics for tilted market portfolios over the same period.

The return differences between tilted market portfolios and the corresponding investable market index are even sharper. In developed markets, the average MSCI IMI return is 0.49 percent per month. The tilted market portfolio earns 0.67 percent per month, a difference of 0.18 percent with a t-statistic of 3.76. In emerging markets, EAFE, and ACWI ex-US, the return differences are 0.16 percent, 0.18 percent and 0.19 percent respectively, with t-statistics of 1.27, 3.67 and 3.37. The inference is quite clear: modest tilts to the joint distribution of value and profitability have generated return premia.

## 4. Conclusions

Value strategies are anchored to a stock variable (book value) whereas profitability strategies are linked to the flow (expected profitability). There is clear economic intuition that the two are related. We study the joint distribution of value and profitability in developed and emerging markets, as well as combinations thereof. Portfolios formed at the intersection of value and profitability generate monotonic return patterns; value and high profitability portfolios substantially outperform growth and low profitability portfolios. For long-only investors with long-horizons, the data suggest considerable benefits to targeting value and profitability jointly rather than individually.

## Appendix

Our data extraction and cleansing procedures generally follow Bessembinder et al. (2019) but with some modifications. A general description follows. Readers interested in a precise replication are encouraged to read the Data Appendix in Bessembinder at al. (2019). The sample consists of securities traded in the developed and emerging markets as identified by MSCI [(https://www.msci.com/market-classification)](https://www.msci.com/market-classification). For Canadian firms, accounting data are extracted from the Compustat data library “compann\_cad”, supplemented with “compgd\_quart”. For non-Canadian firms, annual accounting data are obtained from the “compgd\_ann”, and quarterly accounting data from the “compgd\_quart” file. Firm-level and security-level descriptions are derived from “company\_descrp” and “security\_descrp” respectively. Non-Canadian returns obtained from the “stock\_global” file. Exchange rates are from the “ref\_exchg” file. Our empirical work requires monthly returns. For Canadian firms, local currency monthly returns are obtained from the “stock\_canada” file. These are converted to US dollar returns using daily exchange rates (per US dollar). Market capitalization is similarly converted to US dollars. For non-Canadian firms, there is no monthly return file. We construct monthly returns from daily returns using the latest date in the month with non-zero closing prices and requiring at least five daily observations with positive closing prices. If there are less than 6 monthly return observations, the stock is dropped entirely. Monthly returns greater than 80% and less than -90%, likely due to data errors, are removed. To isolate common stocks, we require that the Compustat Issue Type be equal to 0, and that the security be traded in the primary exchange of the country. There are two exceptions to this rule: China (for which we retain stocks trade in both Shanghai and Shenzhen), and India (for which we retain stocks traded on the Bombay Stock Exchange and the National Stock Exchange). These requirements exclude securities traded in OTC and unlisted markets. We exclude issues outside the country of domicile to avoid double counting of stocks. We use the “EXCHG” field to exclude securities that are not common stocks. Our analysis requires accounting data. In particular, we require the book value of equity (adjusted for goodwill) to construct book-to-market ratios, as well as balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow statement information to construct cash based operating profitability. To construct book equity, we follow standard procedures using shareholder equity, the redemption, liquidating and carrying value of preferred stock (in that order), and subtracting goodwill. We also construct book equity without subtracting goodwill but do not report results from that measure in the body of the paper. Since we use Compustat files, the data elements are similar to those for US markets. Data quality and standardization improve after the introduction of IFRS but we note that since we use largely “high-level” accounting items, distortions are minimal. There are two ways to construct cash based operating profitability (see the Appendix in Ball et al. (2016)). The first adjusts operating profitability using changes in balance sheet items. The second adjusts operating profitability using cash flow statement items. We construct both but report results from the former since balance sheet items are widely available in international markets. We can report that using one or the other does not make a difference to the results.
