# Multi-Objective Maritime Cargo Routing and Scheduling

The charterer's planning problem is intrinsically multi-objective: shipping a commodity from several origin ports to several delivery ports over a fleet of chartered bulk carriers involves **three business objectives that trade off against each other**, and a single weighted sum hides the trade-off.

## Core Structure

- **Objectives** — (i) fleet **makespan** (horizon length, α with $Z_1 \ge t^A_{if}$); (ii) **freight cost** (chartering fee proportional to arrival time, port facility fees, time-window penalties, demurrage from late port departures); (iii) **delivery delays** for the products the charterer monitors at priority ports.
- **Constraints** — time windows per product-port visit; per-vessel draft limits per port; berth limits; per-compartment product separation (bulk commodities cannot be mixed); per-vessel minimum load and per-product minimum load; route structure where all pickups precede all deliveries; no two vessels in the same port simultaneously.
- **Route topology** — $P, P, \dots, P, D, D, \dots, D$: load everything first, then discharge. Split loads across compartments, no transshipment, open routes from dummy start depot $s$ to dummy finish depot $f$.
- **Solution shape** — a **Pareto front** over $(Z_1, Z_2, Z_3)$, generated by sweeping scalarization weights, plus a way to compare fronts when the true front is unknown (hypervolume, $C$-metric).
- **Validated frontier** — ganjia tunes NSGA-II (pop 50, CR 0.5, MR 0.4) and MOPSO (pop 50, w 0.6) by a Taguchi L27 design, reports average gap vs the $\varepsilon$-constraint front of 0.002% cost / 0.008% tardiness for NSGA-II and 0.020% / 0.055% for MOPSO, and in 50 iterations takes cost 126.2 → 69.3 MLCU (45%) and tardiness 23,950 → 858 h (96%), where the exact $\varepsilon$-constraint method itself fails on 2 of 15 experiments at 23–25 customers. The deferral-caveat figures (cost gap $\le$0.62% vs 3.25%, 47% hypervolume edge, 7% cost / 16% CO$_2$ reduction, fuzzy weighted max-min) are **not present in the ganjia source** — ganjia builds the front with $\varepsilon$-constraint and lists fuzzy approaches as future work, and reports no CO$_2$ percentages. ^[inferred]
- **Ganjia et al. add an emission objective**: a bi-objective supply-chain scheduling MINLP coupling single-machine production scheduling, delivery **due-date assignment** (due dates are decision variables; tardiness $T_{del,j} \ge T_{tra,j} - DD_j$), and batch delivery to a heterogeneous VRP with time windows, minimizing freight + emission cost against tardiness [[references/ganjia2026-multiobjective-sustainable-supply-chain-scheduling]]. The emission term is linear in vehicle weight + load and quadratic in average speed $\times$ distance.

## Why It Matters

- Cost and service level are not the same lever: the cheapest schedule is usually the slowest, and the fastest is the one that pays demurrage. A front makes the trade visible.
- The direction-change fee in charter contracts is a concrete example of a business rule that changes the optimum — 75% of the company's own routes changed direction, and fee discounts change cost by up to 13.6% [[references/dossantos2024-multiobjective-maritime-cargo-routing]]. ^[inferred]
- Scalarizing weights with fuzzy membership turns the "which objective matters most?" question into a policy input a manager can read, instead of an internal solver parameter.
- Compared with maritime inventory routing (which optimizes transport cost with inventory as a sunk cost), this setting puts delivery timeliness on the objective list — closer to a charterer's KPIs than to a vertically integrated producer's [[concepts/maritime-inventory-routing-problem]]. ^[inferred]
- Orientation weights act as a policy dial in the ganjia front too: economic orientation 74.52 MLCU cost / 9.43 h tardiness vs customer orientation 2.17 h / 245.11 MLCU, compromise 112.98 / 5.29 h — the same manager-readable preference dial as fuzzy $\theta$ weights, without the membership machinery. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- How do emission objectives change the front? Ganjia's front folds emission cost into the freight objective, not a third axis — a standalone CO$_2$ axis with IMO carbon-intensity targets is open. ^[inferred]
- What does the front look like under stochastic travel, port, and demand times? ^[inferred]
- Can evolutionary multi-objective metaheuristics find a denser front than a weight sweep, and does denser matter when the decision-maker picks few plans? ^[inferred]
