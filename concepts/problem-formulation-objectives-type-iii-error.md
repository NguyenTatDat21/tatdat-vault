> [!tldr] Chapter 3 opens with two epigraphs: "An approximate answer to the right question is worth a great deal more than a precise answer to the wrong question" (the first golden rule of applied mathematics) and Einstein's "The formulation of a problem is often more essential than its solution which may be merely a matter of mathematical or experimental skill."^[extracted]

## Summary

The first step in any statistical investigation is to get a clear understanding of the physical background to the situation under study, to clarify the objectives, and to formulate the problem in statistical terms. "Do not underestimate the time and effort required for this stage of the investigation."^[extracted]

## Key ideas

- **The three first-step bullets, verbatim:** "get a clear understanding of the physical background to the situation under study"; "clarify the objectives"; "formulate the problem in statistical terms".^[extracted]
- **Asking questions is the mechanism.** In a consultative or collaborative project the statistician must be prepared to ask lots of questions; all variables thought to be important need to be listed, and distinctive features of the system such as constraints and known limiting behaviour should be noted.^[extracted]
- **Objectives are the hard part.** They may range from a general desire for more information and understanding, perhaps via an exploratory study, to a specific aim of testing a particular hypothesis, fitting a particular model, or choosing a course of action from a predetermined list. "It is always a good idea to think exactly how the results of a study will actually be used."^[extracted]
- **Type III error.** The objectives may be unclear even to the person who asked for help, or may turn out to be quite different from those initially suggested. "Giving the 'right' answer to the wrong question is a more common error than might be expected and is sometimes called a type III error or an error of the third kind (Kimball, 1957). Thus finding the right question may be harder than finding the right answer."^[extracted]
- **No unique answer under ill-defined objectives.** The solution may depend critically on the assumptions, so there may be a range of possible solutions rather than a single answer. Textbook problems have neat analytic solutions; many real problems do not.^[extracted]
- **Near-optimality over exact optimality.** "More guidance is needed on how to find good, but not necessarily optimal, solutions since near-optimality over a range of conditions is much more useful to the practitioner than exact optimality which is valid only under strict, possibly artificial, assumptions."^[extracted]
- **Cost and prior information are preliminary questions.** Many projects are under-funded as well as under-planned, and it may be necessary to say that the targets are unobtainable with the resources available.^[extracted]
- **The literature search can remove the need for the study.** "The author was once asked to cooperate in conducting a sample survey to investigate a particular problem related to medical care, but half an hour in the library produced a published report on a previous survey which made the new survey redundant."^[extracted]
- **Existing laws change the question.** Where well-established laws exist (Boyle's law in physics, laws relating market penetration and amount bought in marketing research), the main question about new data is whether they agree with existing laws. "It is unfortunate that most statistics textbooks devote overwhelming attention to treating data as if they are always brand-new one-off samples."^[extracted]
- **The key question about a model** is "What is the model going to be used for?", whose answer determines whether to go for a simple or a complicated model and what level of descriptive accuracy is required.^[extracted]
- **The teaching critique.** Most teaching exercises ask for solutions to ready-made, artificially clear-cut questions, so students get little practice at formulating problems and objectives "and may end up learning the hard way, by making mistakes in real-life situations. This is a shame!"^[extracted]
- **Further reading:** Hand (1994) on problem formulation under the name "deconstructing statistical questions"; Hamaker (1983) on industrial experiments where an initial interrogation showed that background information was needed, or that no experiment was required at all.^[extracted]
